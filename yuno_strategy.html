The PayLink Ultimatum: Should Yuno Partner or Compete in Indonesia?
Decision required within two weeks. Three options modelled. Clear recommendation below.
Executive Summary
Context

Indonesia is the next strategic market for Yuno, but we have a near-term gap in local payment coverage. We must decide in two weeks whether to partner with PayLink to launch in ~60 days — fast and broad coverage, but lower margins and a 3-year exclusivity lock-in — or continue building our own direct integrations, which offer better economics and control but will take 6–9 more months. The urgency is that three key merchants are pressuring for Indonesia by Q3 2024 and may switch to competitors if we miss the window. The core tradeoff is speed and merchant retention now versus margin and flexibility later, with PayLink's approval-rate advantage still unproven.

Recommendation: accept PayLink's proposal, but negotiate the exclusivity down to 12–18 months. Use PayLink to launch Indonesia in 60 days, protecting the three at-risk merchants, while Yuno's own direct integrations continue in parallel. By Year 2, begin transitioning volume onto Yuno's stack at full economics.

The 3-year deal as written is too expensive. PayLink takes 65% of revenue, dropping Yuno's effective take rate from ~0.20% to ~0.07% — nearly 3x worse. On $550M of GMV in Year 3 alone, that gap costs around $700K in foregone revenue. And three years of exclusivity means no routing optimization and no leverage over PayLink for the entire growth phase of what could be Yuno's largest Asian market — which is Yuno's core product.

But walking away entirely is equally risky. The three merchants represent $360K in existing annual revenue and could pull their whole Yuno relationship, not just Indonesia. One has already started a pilot with Pagoda Payments. Waiting another six months to build direct is a real churn risk.

The bridge solves both problems: Indonesia goes live in 60 days, the merchants stay, and Yuno only gives up the better economics for one year — not three. The numbers confirm it. Bridge NPV over three years is $1.63M, versus $1.38M for the full PayLink exclusive and $1.55M for the direct build.

What the decision hinges on
  1. Exclusivity flexibility. If PayLink will not cap the lock-in to 12–18 months, the bridge is not available and the choice narrows to accepting a structurally damaging deal or building direct and managing the churn risk aggressively.
  2. Merchant churn type. The three at-risk accounts could exit Yuno entirely (the $417K per merchant worst case) or simply find another Indonesia solution while staying on Yuno for LATAM and Europe. That distinction changes the financial case for Direct Build significantly.
  3. Direct build execution confidence. The product team estimates 6 more months to feature parity. If that slips to 9–12 months, the bridge period needs to be extended and the economics shift further toward partnership.

Non-negotiable walk-away condition: If PayLink insists on three-year blanket exclusivity with no performance-based exit rights, decline the deal. The cost of routing lock-in over 36 months in Indonesia's growth phase outweighs the merchant retention value of the partnership under any reasonable scenario.

Strategic Analysis
Exclusivity risk, approval rate assessment, competitive context
▾
The core question

Yuno's Indonesia entry is constrained by a narrow window. Three enterprise merchants need Indonesia coverage by Q3 2024 or they will evaluate alternatives — one is already piloting Pagoda Payments, a direct Yuno competitor. PayLink solves this in 60 days. Building direct takes nine months from today.

The real tradeoff is not about PayLink's API catalog. It is whether Yuno should trade strategic control and long-term unit economics for speed-to-market in a high-barrier market. A 3-year exclusive partnership effectively converts Indonesia into a single-provider resale model, eliminating Yuno's ability to differentiate on routing, optimization, and merchant experience in what could become one of its largest markets.

Why the exclusivity clause matters more than the revenue share

The 35/65 revenue split is painful — Yuno's effective take rate drops from ~0.20% to ~0.07%, approximately 2.9x worse. But that is a recoverable problem once direct integrations are live. Exclusivity is not.

  • Single-provider concentration. Any PayLink outage, pricing change, or regulatory constraint affects Yuno's entire Indonesia stack. This is the opposite of what orchestration is supposed to deliver.
  • No routing intelligence. Yuno's commercial pitch is intelligent multi-PSP routing for better approval rates and lower cost. Indonesia becomes the market where that pitch does not apply.
  • Leverage shifts permanently. Once exclusive, PayLink can deprioritize Yuno's roadmap requests, raise fees, or withhold performance data, because Yuno cannot credibly threaten to reroute volume.
  • Stranded internal investment. The team is already 40% through the direct build — six months of engineering work. A three-year lock-in defers the value of that investment and delays Indonesia capability-building at the worst possible time.
PayLink's approval rate claim: credible but unverifiable

PayLink claims 4–6 percentage points higher approval rates than alternatives. This is plausible given their deeper local integrations, but they have provided only aggregated benchmarks — not transaction-level data. The claim should not be accepted at face value in commercial terms.

More importantly, the approval rate advantage does not materially change Yuno's P&L. At a 0.07% net take rate, a 5pp approval uplift on $100M of attempted GMV increases Yuno's revenue by roughly $3.5K per year. It matters far more to merchants (incremental sales) than it does to Yuno's financials. To make up for the 2.9x revenue share reduction through approval rates alone, PayLink would need to generate roughly 2.9x more Indonesia GMV — not the 5–6% improvement approvals deliver.

The approval advantage is a merchant retention argument, not a unit economics argument. If it accelerates merchant adoption in Year 1, that justifies the bridge. It does not justify a 36-month lock-in.

Verification before signing: require six months of rolling transaction-level approval data segmented by payment method, issuer bank, and amount range, plus the right to run A/B routing tests with at least one of the at-risk merchants. If PayLink refuses, the claim is a negotiating position.

Competitive context: Pagoda Payments

Pagoda entered Indonesia 18 months ago using a hybrid model — some direct integrations, some aggregator relationships — and is reportedly growing fast. One Yuno merchant has already started a pilot with them.

The Pagoda threat is real but not invincible. If Yuno executes the bridge well — Indonesia live in 60 days, direct integrations by Month 12, routing optimization by Month 18 — Yuno arrives at a structurally stronger position than Pagoda: deeper routing intelligence, a proven enterprise merchant base in LATAM and Europe, and broader global coverage. The risk is that a 6–9 month delay lets Pagoda lock in the accounts that matter.

Anchor the negotiation around a bridge-and-transition structure rather than arguing over revenue share alone. Give PayLink a credible GMV commitment from the three at-risk merchants and co-marketing positioning as Yuno's inaugural Indonesia partner. If PayLink refuses a bridge — fallback plan

If PayLink insists on three-year blanket exclusivity, do not sign. Instead:

  • Ship an Indonesia MVP in 8–12 weeks covering the methods that drive the majority of enterprise GMV: one or two major e-wallets, virtual accounts, and cards. Not 30 methods on day one — good enough to retain the merchants who matter.
  • Use short-term, non-exclusive contracts with one or two local PSPs (Xendit, Midtrans) for method coverage. Avoid any aggregator demanding exclusivity.
  • Offer the three at-risk merchants a temporary commercial incentive — reduced take rate or service credits — in exchange for a written six-month commitment not to switch their full stack.
  • Publish a dated Indonesia roadmap to all enterprise accounts expanding into SEA. Proactively message the pipeline before Pagoda does.

Key uncertainties to resolve before signing
  • Definition of "net take rate" in the revenue share. Confirm whether PayLink's 65% is calculated on Yuno's net margin after underlying costs, or whether PayLink sets those underlying costs. If the latter, Yuno's effective economics could compress further.
  • Local regulatory requirements for Yuno. Validate whether Yuno needs any Bank Indonesia registrations or if integrating through a licensed PSP is sufficient. If local licensing is required, the direct build timeline could extend materially.
  • PayLink operational maturity. Uptime history, reconciliation accuracy, refund success rates, dispute handling, and settlement timeliness matter as much as approval rates for merchant satisfaction. These have not been assessed.
  • Internal execution risk. PayLink integration can consume the same engineering bandwidth needed to finish direct integrations. Ring-fencing the direct build team is essential and must be an explicit decision from day one.
  • Exclusivity and enterprise redundancy requirements. Some enterprise merchants require multi-PSP redundancy for business continuity. A single-provider Indonesia stack may be a commercial blocker for certain verticals regardless of approval rates.
PayLink due diligence checklist
  • Licensing & regulatory: copies of Bank Indonesia licenses, compliance audit reports, any regulatory actions in the last 24 months
  • Approval rate verification: six months of transaction-level outcome data (BIN/issuer-level), segmented by payment method, merchant category, and amount band
  • Technical: API documentation, webhook reliability, idempotency guarantees, p95/p99 latency, uptime SLOs and historic uptime, error codes, SDK availability
  • Reconciliation: file formats, settlement timing, mismatch rates, dispute flows, reporting granularity
  • Commercial: full fee schedule including FX markup, payout fees, reserves, minimums, and pricing guarantee (MFN clause)
  • Security & data: SOC2/ISO certification, encryption standards, sub-processors, breach notification terms, data retention and export policies
  • Roadmap: how new payment rails are added, lead times, deprecation policies, and sandbox environment for ongoing development
